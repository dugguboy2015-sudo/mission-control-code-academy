MISSION 7.1: WHAT AI/ML/LLMs ACTUALLY ARE

Tokens, Prediction, No Magic

Everything you've built through Sector 6 was deterministic — the same input always produces the exact same output, forever. An LLM (Large Language Model) breaks that rule on purpose. It's not thinking, it doesn't "know" things the way a database does, and it can be confidently, fluently wrong. Before you write a single line of code that calls one, you need an honest, no-magic mental model of what's actually happening inside — because every good decision in the rest of this sector depends on it.

1

Briefing

Strip away the marketing language and an LLM does exactly one thing, over and over: given some text, predict the single most statistically likely next chunk of text, then repeat with that chunk appended, then repeat again. That's it. There's no separate "thinking" step, no lookup into a database of facts, no understanding in the way a person understands. It's pattern-completion, at a massive scale, trained on an enormous amount of human-written text — which is precisely why it's often right (most text about a topic agrees on the facts) and occasionally, fluently wrong (it's still just completing a pattern, not checking a source).

This loop — predict, pick, append, repeat — is the entire mechanism. Every single word of every LLM response was produced this way, one token at a time.
2

Field Manual

Tokens — Not Words, Not Letters

LLMs don't process text one letter or even one whole word at a time — they process tokens, chunks somewhere in between. A common word like "the" is usually one token. A rarer or longer word might get split into several: "unbelievable" might become un + believ + able. This matters for a reason you'll use directly in Mission 7.8: every provider charges by the token, not by the word or character, so unusual words, code, and non-English text often cost more than plain English of the same length.

How Prediction Actually Works

At every single step, the model doesn't just pick "the best next word" — it computes a probability for every possible next token in its entire vocabulary (tens of thousands of options), then samples one, weighted by those probabilities. Most of the time the highest-probability option wins, which is why output usually looks coherent — but it's still a roll of a very loaded die, not a certainty.

Temperature — Dialing Randomness Up or Down

Temperature is a setting you control when calling most LLM APIs, and it directly controls that sampling step. At temperature 0, the model always picks the single highest-probability token — fully deterministic, same input, same output, every time. At a higher temperature, lower-probability options get picked more often too, producing more varied, "creative" output — at the cost of consistency and, past a point, coherence.

WHEN TO USE WHICH Low temperature suits tasks with one right answer — extracting a date from text, classifying a support ticket, generating code. Higher temperature suits creative tasks — brainstorming names, writing varied practice dialogue. There's no universally "correct" temperature; it's a deliberate trade-off you choose per use case.

Why LLMs "Hallucinate"

A hallucination is when a model states something false with the exact same fluent confidence as something true. This isn't a bug that a future update simply removes — it's a direct consequence of how prediction works. The model isn't checking a fact database; it's producing the most statistically plausible continuation of the text so far. Most of the time, "statistically plausible" and "actually true" line up, because most training text is accurate. But nothing in the mechanism guarantees it — which is exactly why Sector 8's Evals mission exists: for anything that matters, you verify the output instead of trusting it blindly.

Training vs. Your Conversation

"Training" is a separate, one-time (or periodically repeated) process where the model reads an enormous amount of text and adjusts its internal parameters — it is NOT reading your conversation as it happens. Once trained, the model itself doesn't change or "remember" you between separate conversations. Anything it needs to know about your specific conversation has to be re-sent as text in the context window every single time — a concept Mission 7.9 covers in full.

⚠ COMMON MISTAKES

  • Assuming an LLM "looked something up" when it stated a fact — it produced a plausible continuation, which may or may not be accurate.
  • Expecting identical output on every call without setting temperature to 0 — some randomness is the default behavior of most APIs.
  • Treating a fluent, confident answer as automatically a correct one — fluency and accuracy are not the same thing for a next-token predictor.
  • Assuming the model "remembers" a previous, separate conversation — without it being resent as context, it's gone.
