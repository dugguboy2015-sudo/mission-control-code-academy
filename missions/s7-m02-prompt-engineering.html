MISSION 7.2: PROMPT ENGINEERING FUNDAMENTALS

Talking to the Model Precisely

Mission 7.1 established that an LLM predicts the most statistically plausible continuation of the text you give it. That single fact has a direct, practical consequence: the exact wording, structure, and framing of your input — the prompt — is the single biggest lever you have over the quality of what comes back. A vague prompt narrows nothing; a precise one narrows the model toward exactly what you actually wanted. This is a real engineering skill, learned by writing, testing, and refining — not a lucky guess.

Field Manual

System vs User Messages

The system message sets standing instructions and persona that apply to the whole conversation — tone, role, constraints. The user message is the specific request for this turn. Separating them means you don't have to repeat "respond concisely, as a friendly tutor" in every single message — say it once in the system message, and it shapes everything that follows.

Being Specific Narrows the Possibilities

"Tell me about dogs" could reasonably be answered a thousand different ways — history, breeds, care tips, biology — and the model has to guess which one you meant, usually landing on something generic. "List 3 low-shedding dog breeds good for apartments, one sentence each" leaves almost no ambiguity about what a good answer looks like.

WHY THIS WORKS, MECHANICALLY Every extra specific detail in your prompt shifts the probability distribution over what comes next — Mission 7.1's exact mechanism. Vague input keeps that distribution wide and generic; specific input concentrates it toward what you actually asked for.

Few-Shot Examples: Show, Don't Just Tell

Describing a format in words is often less reliable than simply showing 1-3 examples of exactly the input/output shape you want, right inside the prompt — called few-shot prompting. The model pattern-matches the shape of your examples far more consistently than it interprets an abstract description of that shape.

PROMPT TEXT
Convert each sentence to past tense.

Example: "I eat breakfast." -> "I ate breakfast."
Example: "She runs fast." -> "She ran fast."

Now convert: "They build a robot."
WHAT JUST HAPPENED Two examples establish a clear pattern (present tense in, past tense out, arrow format) before the real request. This is called "few-shot" as opposed to "zero-shot" (no examples at all) — and it's one of the most reliable ways to lock in a consistent output shape.

Asking for a Specific Output Format

If you need the response in a particular shape — a numbered list, exactly one sentence, a specific tone — ask for it explicitly and specifically. "Respond in exactly 3 bullet points, no more than 10 words each" leaves far less room for a surprising reply than "keep it short."

Iterating: Prompt Engineering Is a Loop, Not a Guess

Nobody writes the perfect prompt on the first try. The real skill is a loop: write a prompt, run it, look closely at what came back, identify exactly what went wrong (too long? wrong format? missed a detail?), and adjust one thing at a time. Treat a disappointing first response as data, not failure — it tells you precisely what to make more specific next.

⚠ COMMON MISTAKES

  • Writing a vague prompt and being surprised the reply is generic — vagueness in the input directly produces vagueness in the output.
  • Describing a desired format in abstract words instead of just showing an example of it.
  • Repeating the same persona/tone instructions in every single user message instead of setting them once in the system message.
  • Giving up after one disappointing response instead of treating it as information about what to make more specific.
